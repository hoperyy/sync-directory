A practical pricing guide

How to price a 3D print

A useful quote starts with a cost model you can explain. Material is one line in that model. Your time, equipment, failed attempts and recurring overhead also need a place.

1. Start with the complete material consumption

Use the total grams or milliliters from your slicer, including supports and purge. Divide the package price by its usable contents and multiply by the amount consumed. Keep mass and volume units consistent; a 1 kg resin bottle needs a density-based conversion before you treat it as mL.

2. Give machine time a rate

One approach is (printer purchase price − expected resale value + expected maintenance) ÷ expected productive hours. A printer costing 600 with 100 of expected resale and 100 of maintenance across 2,000 productive hours gives 0.30 per hour. These figures are an illustration, not a recommended depreciation schedule.

3. Measure power and hands-on labor separately

Average watts × hours ÷ 1,000 gives energy in kWh. Multiply by your electricity tariff. Hands-on labor includes setup, slicing, loading, support removal, cleaning and packaging. Automated printing time belongs in machine time; it is not automatically hands-on labor.

4. Allow for repeats

If a full attempt has a failure probability p, expected attempts for one successful print are 1 ÷ (1 − p). At a 10% failure rate that is about 1.111 attempts. This model repeats material, power and machine costs. It assumes independent attempts, a constant failure rate and full-cost failures. Use your job history to revise the assumption.

5. Choose margin with care

For a cost of 10, a 30% markup gives 13. A 30% gross margin gives 10 ÷ 0.70 = 14.29, because profit is measured as a fraction of the selling price. Neither number automatically covers tax, marketplace fees, shipping or other overhead. Include allocations for them when setting costs.

6. Allocate shared-batch costs

If five identical parts use a total of ten machine hours and 30 minutes of hands-on work, a simple equal allocation gives two hours and six minutes per part. Enter those per-item values before selecting a quantity of five. For mixed jobs, allocate shared costs using an appropriate basis such as machine time or material usage.

7. Compare estimates with completed jobs

Record actual material, machine time and hands-on effort for completed prints. Revise the rates and failure assumption when the estimate consistently misses. The calculator can export CSV and save a profile in your browser.
