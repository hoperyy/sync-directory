A practical pricing guide

How to price a 3D print

A useful quote starts with a cost model you can explain. Material is one line in that model. Your time, equipment, failed attempts and recurring overhead also need a place.

1. Start with the complete material consumption

Use total grams or milliliters from your slicer, including supports and purge. Keep units consistent; convert a resin bottle’s mass to volume using its manufacturer’s density.

2. Give machine time a rate

One approach is (printer purchase price − expected resale value + expected maintenance) ÷ expected productive hours. A printer costing 600 with 100 of resale and 100 of maintenance over 2,000 productive hours gives 0.30 per hour. These are example assumptions.

3. Separate successful work, setup and retry labor

Successful labor covers work around a completed item or plate. One-off order setup is allocated across the order once. Retry labor is extra effort for a failed attempt; it is weighted by the expected number of failed attempts, p ÷ (1 − p). Avoid counting the same work in two fields.

4. Choose the correct batch basis

Per-item mode multiplies item costs by quantity. Whole-plate mode divides entered plate totals equally across identical items. It does not predict plate capacity, material savings or print time: get those totals from your slicer. A plate using 150 g, five machine hours and 20 minutes of successful labor across five identical items allocates 30 g, one hour and four minutes per item, before setup and retry costs.

5. Model full-cost failures deliberately

At failure probability p, expected attempts are 1 ÷ (1 − p). Material, power and machine time repeat. The assumption is constant, independent, full-cost failures; whole-plate mode assumes a failed plate must be repeated. Use separate estimates when partial failures make that model inappropriate.

6. Account for selling fees before margin

For production cost 10, a 30% margin without fees gives 10 ÷ 0.70 = 14.29. A 5% selling fee raises it to 10 ÷ 0.65 = 15.38. Add an allocated fixed order fee to the numerator. Profit after entered fees divided by pre-tax price is the margin shown. Fees on tax or shipping, tax collection and shipping costs need separate treatment.

7. Test a price, then make a quote

The calculator shows profit and margin at your own proposed price. Pin estimates to compare assumptions on this page. Its customer quote shows the item price, quantity and estimated total; internal cost rates stay in your worksheet. Final commercial terms still need agreement with your customer.
